01 · Instrumentation

Respiratory impedance monitor for non-invasive ventilation

Optik Consultancy, UTS Tech Lab
Client: Respiratory Investigation Unit, Royal North Shore Hospital
May to August 2026 · Electrical and measurement engineer

A six person multidisciplinary team. I owned the electrical and measurement side: excitation firmware, the measurement engine, and the calibration methodology.

Two earlier student groups had tried this problem for the hospital and failed for basic reasons. Genuine electrical capability was the bottleneck, and that is what I was there to supply.

The device applies a Forced Oscillation Technique signal on top of non-invasive ventilation and measures respiratory impedance while the patient breathes normally, so that expiratory flow limitation in COPD can be detected without interrupting therapy. I built the excitation firmware and the measurement engine that turns pressure and flow signals into respiratory resistance and reactance, selected and bench verified the differential pressure transducer against its amplitude and phase response, and developed the calibration methodology.

It is the first working measurement a student group has brought back to the hospital on this problem. I demonstrated it to the clinical team and told them directly that the patient side measurement was not yet accurate enough, and why.

02 · Forecasting

Electricity price forecasting for the National Electricity Market

Engineering Capstone, UTS · Supervisor: Dr Gang Lei
February to November 2026 · Solo project

Entirely my own work, across two semesters. Built on AEMO five minute dispatch price data for New South Wales.

Four model families, ranked only by how close they get to the price that actually settled.

I built and compared XGBoost, LSTM, ARIMA and LEAR day ahead forecasters under a common evaluation protocol, on 1 April to 24 August 2026. XGBoost was the most accurate, at an average error of $17.80 per MWh against the actual spot price. I deliberately rank models against the settled price rather than against a naive benchmark, because a favourable benchmark can make a weak model look strong.

A separate five minute ahead model runs on the same data and reaches a mean absolute error of 6.42 $/MWh, and unlike the day ahead models it can predict above $300. The two are different forecasting tasks and I do not compare their errors with each other.

The honest limitation. The models find the shape of a spike day but not its height. That gap is the whole point of the second semester, which extends the work to probabilistic forecasting and to features extracted from AEMO’s own written price spike reports.
03 · Transmission

Battery storage placement on the WSCC 9 bus system

Power Systems Studio A, UTS · High Distinction
February to May 2026 · Team of three

All three of us worked every part of the study in parallel rather than splitting it into sections. The results below are the team’s. The PSS®E automation was mine.

One bus was in violation. Putting 50 MW of storage at that bus was worth more than putting it anywhere else.

We modelled the WSCC 9 bus benchmark in Siemens PSS®E and ran load flow, N-1 contingency screening, P-V voltage stability and three phase fault transient analysis against NER S5.1a and S5.1.4 and AEMO stability guidance. Under loss of Line 4-5, Bus 5 fell to 0.8389 pu, the only bus outside limits. A 50 MW battery at Bus 5, modelled with the REGCA1 and REECA1 converter models, lifted it to 0.9411 pu and displaced 50.3 MW from the slack generator.

We compared four candidate buses, 4, 5, 7 and 9, on P-V margin. With the battery at Bus 5, collapse free transfer under loss of Line 4-5 rose from 200 MW to 320 MW. I wrote the psspy and dyntools pipeline that loads the case, sweeps fault clearing times in a loop, pulls the channels back out and plots them, which turned a thirty minute manual sweep into a few seconds.

0.8389 to 0.9411 pu
Transfer margin
200 to 320 MW, Line 4-5 out

psspy, dyntools, matplotlib
What the battery did not do. It did not improve critical clearing time. The transient study showed the CCT is set by the machines and the fault location, and the battery does not change it, so we did not claim an improvement we could not demonstrate.
04 · Protection

Six zone protection scheme for a 22 kV industrial customer network

Power Systems Studio B, UTS · Brief supplied by Middleton Group
August to November 2025 · Individually authored, peer verified

Three of us each designed the complete scheme independently, then used each other’s designs to check our own settings.

Other teams ran it like a relay, one person per section of the network. All three of us ran the same track from start to finish, sharing the bottle of water.

A 2 MVA Dyn11 transformer supplying 1.6 MVA across three LV feeders. I designed six coordinated protection zones: transformer differential on an SEL 387-1A, busbar differential on an SEL 487E-1A, and overcurrent and earth fault protection on each feeder to IEC 60255 inverse time curves with a 0.3 second grading margin to the upstream backup relay. CT ratios and polarities were selected so that relay secondary currents stayed within the 1 A rating. The whole scheme was validated in DIgSILENT PowerFactory across three phase, two phase, single line to ground and two phase to ground faults at multiple locations.

While tracing the zones I found that the LV busbar was not covered by any of them. I raised it with the academic supervisor, who confirmed it sat outside the brief but recommended designing it anyway, so I added the busbar differential zone.

05 · Microgrid

Sustainable hybrid microgrid for a remote site in Mildura

Professional Studio A, UTS · High Distinction
August to November 2025 · Team of six · 66 hours logged

My share of the work was the battery and its DC to DC converter, plus the bus architecture decision. The system level results are the team’s.

I argued the team out of a DC bus, and I brought published sources rather than a preference.

A 10 kW solar array, a 2 kW micro hydro turbine and a 50 kWh battery supplying three houses, modelled in MATLAB and Simulink with Simscape Electrical and Simscape Battery. I modelled the battery and its bidirectional buck and boost DC to DC converter.

The team started on a DC bus architecture. I read the published microgrid literature independently and proposed switching to a common AC bus, because the micro hydro generator and the household loads are both natively AC and a DC bus forced conversion stages that earned nothing. The team adopted it, and it removed a whole layer of integration work.

06 · Power electronics

Synchronous buck converter, 40 V to 10 V at 35 W

Power Electronics 41278, UTS · High Distinction, 87
Semester 1 2025 · Team of four

I designed and wound the inductor and ran the efficiency optimisation. Teammates covered the mathematical proof, the LTspice simulation, the control implementation and the literature review.

The converter worked on the first build. It was just wasting a third of the input power, and the part everyone had trusted was the problem.

The first build converted correctly but ran at 68.9 per cent efficiency. The supplied 470 microhenry ferrite choke was both the wrong inductance for the design and a poor core, so it saturated and drew excess current. I built a test circuit to measure saturation current and used an LCR meter to characterise candidates, then wound a 300 microhenry inductor on a plastic bobbin, 40 turns with a paper air gap, chosen to hold above 5 A without saturating and to have lower winding resistance than the supplied part.

With the new inductor and a rebuilt breadboard layout that shortened the high current loops, the converter reached 90.62 per cent efficiency at 40 V in, 9.976 V out and 3.49 A, under closed loop PI control at 50 kHz on an Arduino Uno.

Approach And what I work with

How I work.

I am a final year Electrical and Electronic Engineering (Honours) student at UTS, completing in November 2026, and I am aiming for a career in power systems. I like problems that force me to work across hardware, software and the physical bench at the same time.

Two habits show up in every project on this page. The first is that I check a result before I report it, and I have been burned enough to know that a test passing is not the same as a design being correct. The second is that when a number is a team result, or is not yet verified, I label it that way rather than letting it read as mine.

I have been the person who found the gap nobody had scoped, on the LV busbar in the protection project, and the person who told a hospital its device was not accurate enough yet. Both of those were more useful than staying quiet would have been.
